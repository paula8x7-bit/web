Short haul cabin crew: what to eat on earlies and multi sector days

Reports in the middle of the night, four to six sectors with 25 minute turnarounds and, often, back to base. Here's how to plan your food so you still have energy on the last sector.

I'm a nutritionist and an Inflight Manager. I started on short haul: four years of four and up to six sectors a day, 25 minute turnarounds, weeks of earlies and weeks of lates.

We brought our food from home: there was nothing on board for the crew. Sometimes we ate on the ground if we landed early, and if not, in the air after the onboard sales. It depended a lot on the destination.

Not every airline works the same way: some short haul crews night stop and others always return to base. This article is mainly for those there and back days with several sectors in a row.

This is what I wish someone had told me back then, together with what the research on short haul crew says.

By Paula Rubio, integrative nutritionist · November 2026

Why earlies take their toll

In a study of 95 short haul pilots, the night before an early report they slept about an hour and a half less than before a late start, and they began the day more tired.1

And it adds up. In another study of aircrew (cabin crew included), the more very early duties in a week, the more tired they became, and every extra hour of sleep helped.2

Sectors count too: with four flights in a day, fatigue hit its critical point more than two hours earlier than with just one.3

Get organised on your days off

With five days of flying in a row, if your food isn't ready you end up living on sandwiches or instant noodles. What works best is cooking once or twice on your days off and portioning it out.

Pack the next day's bag the night before: breakfast, something to snack on between sectors and your main meal. Nobody makes good food choices at 3:30am.

If you're going to heat your meal in the aircraft oven, pack it in an ovenproof container.

Before you report: a light breakfast

If you report at 5am, your body still thinks it's night: between midnight and 6am, keep it small and easy to digest. In a night shift study, people who had a light snack in the early hours performed better than those who had a full meal or ate nothing.4

The key is not to leave home on an empty stomach and a lot of coffee.

Ideas for that first breakfast

  • Natural yoghurt or kefir with fruit.
  • Sourdough toast with olive oil and tomato.
  • A glass of milk or a plant drink with a few nuts.
  • A boiled egg, made the night before.

Between sectors: food you can eat in five minutes

With a 25 minute turnaround you never know if you'll get to sit down. Pack things you can eat quickly without cutlery, and spread them across the sectors so you don't reach the last one without having eaten.

Have your main meal whenever there's a gap: on a turnaround when you land early, or in the air after the service.

What fits in your bag

  • Boiled eggs.
  • Carrot, cucumber and pepper sticks with hummus.
  • Whole fruit: bananas, apples, satsumas.
  • A handful of nuts.
  • Hard cheese or small tins of tuna or sardines.
  • For your main meal: rice or potatoes, vegetables and protein (chicken, fish, beans or a Spanish omelette).

Fluids and electrolytes through the day

Cabin air is very dry5 and on a busy day it's easy to go hours without drinking. Bring your own bottle and have a little on every sector, without waiting until you're thirsty.

Electrolytes help your body hold on to what you drink. A trial comparing thirteen drinks found that milk and oral rehydration solutions were retained much better than water, while sports drinks were no different from water.6

In practice: have your water with food (fruit, vegetables and something salty, like cheese or tinned fish) and, if you can buy it airside, a yoghurt, a kefir or a glass of milk. On very hot or very busy days, oral rehydration sachets from the pharmacy are an option: the powder goes through security and you mix it with the water in your bottle. Ask a professional how to take them.

The hot water on board

It's what's at hand for a tea, some noodles or something warm, but the water in the aircraft tanks isn't always in good condition. A study that tested water on board found 37 different species of bacteria, and the truck that fills the tanks was one of the main sources.7

A full flask won't get through security, not even as crew. If you like something hot during the day, take it empty and fill it airside. And the food you heat in the aircraft oven doesn't need any water.

Caffeine: early in the day

Coffee helps in the first half of your duty, but it takes hours to clear: even 6 hours before bed, it cuts your sleep short.8

If you're on another early tomorrow, that mid afternoon coffee will take away exactly the hours you need. Have it early, a little at a time, and nothing in the 6 hours before bed.

Back home: a normal meal and an early night

If you finish around midday, have a proper meal, like on any other day. That way you won't spend the evening snacking.

If you're reporting early again tomorrow, bring dinner and bedtime forward. Adding up hours of sleep is what protects you most from a run of earlies.2

Weeks of lates: when you finish after midnight

Late starts look easier, but by the end of the duty you've been awake for a long time, and fatigue on landing is higher than on earlies.9

Have your main meal before you report, something light on the evening sectors and, once home, only something small before bed.

Frequently asked questions

What should I eat before a 5am report?+

Something light and easy to digest: yoghurt or kefir with fruit, toast with olive oil or a glass of milk with a few nuts. At that hour your body is still in its biological night.

What should cabin crew eat on a four or six sector day?+

Food you can eat in five minutes without cutlery, like boiled eggs, fruit, nuts or vegetable sticks with hummus, spread across the sectors. Have your main meal when there's a gap on a turnaround or after the service.

How can I replace electrolytes on a busy duty?+

Have your water with food (fruit, vegetables and something salty) and, if you can buy it airside, a yoghurt or a glass of milk. In one trial, milk and oral rehydration solutions were retained better than water; rehydration sachets in powder form go through security.

Is the hot water on a plane safe to drink?+

The water in aircraft tanks isn't always in good condition: one study found many species of bacteria. For something hot, take an empty flask and fill it airside.

When should I stop caffeine if I have an early tomorrow?+

Have it in the first half of your duty and leave at least 6 hours without caffeine before bed.
